Particles in the Presence of Ultrafine Bubbles

Abstract:Ultrafine bubbles (UFBs) have been proposed as interfacial agents that modulate 1
colloidal interactions, yet their role in early-stage flocculation remains insufficiently quanti- 2
fied. Using amidine latex (AL) as a cationic model colloid under controlled end-over-end 3
mixing, we combined flocculation kinetics with electrokinetic and interfacial measurements 4
to elucidate the mechanism by which UFBs promote aggregation. Electrophoretic measure- 5
ments show adsorption driven charge regulation by bubbles: increasing the UFBs to AL 6
ratio progressively neutralizes the surface and at sufficient dose reverses its charge. The 7
neutrality point occurs at a characteristic ratio that is only weakly sensitive to background 8
sodium chloride (NaCl). Interfacial measurements reveal a thicker hydrodynamic layer at 9
higher ionic strength, consistent with closer packing of adsorbed UFBs under double layer 10
compression, and micrographs of particle dimers confirm a larger inter-particle separation 11
that directly visualizes this layer. Aggregation accelerates at 10 mM sodium chloride but 12
remains slow at 0.1 mM, indicating that electrolyte screening is required for efficient ad- 13
sorption and bridging; pH modulated the process secondarily. Together, the results support 14
a coherent picture in which UFBs adsorption creates patchy, charge-compensated surfaces 15
and a soft hydrodynamic layer that enlarges the effective collision cross section, thereby 16
enhancing early-stage flocculation. 17
Keywords:Colloid particles; ultrafine bubbles; flocculation; end-over-end mixing.18
1. Introduction 19
The rapidly growing interest in ultrafine bubbles (UFBs), defined by ISO 20480-1:2017 20
as gas bubbles with diameters smaller than 1 µm, is commonly discussed alongside surface 21
nanobubbles attached to solid interfaces and bulk nanobubbles dispersed in liquids [ 1,2]. 22
Unlike colloid particles, UFBs exhibit distinctive physicochemical properties, such as 23
exceptionally high specific surface area, low terminal velocity, remarkable stability, efficient 24
gas transfer capabilities, and the potential to generate oxygen radicals in aqueous media [ 3– 25
5]. Owing to their negligible buoyancy, UFBs can remain suspended in water for weeks, 26
during which they modulate viscosity and surface tension. UFBs have been reported 27
toelevate dissolved oxygen and exhibit antibac terialactivity,which isoftenattributed 28
tothegeneration ofreactive oxygen species (ROS) such ashydroxyl radicals (OH) at 29
thegas–waterinterface, lead ingtooxidative stress inmicrobial cells [ 6–14]. Collectively, 30
these attributes underpin both demonstrated and emerging applications across water and 31
wastewater treatment, flotation processes, eutrophication management, phytoremediation, 32

aquaculture, and interface cleaning [ 5,15–18]. Recent reviews have highlighted novel 33
roles for UFBs in biomedical fields e.g., drug delivery, biofilm disruption and energy 34
processes e.g., electrochemical reactions, emphasizing that a comprehensive understanding 35
of UFBs–particle interactions is critical for expanding their utility [2,5].36
A notable characteristic of UFBs is their negatively charged surface, generally at- 37
tributed to the enrichment of hydroxide ions (OH−) at the gas–water interface [ 19–22]. 38
This surface charge strongly influences their interactions with particles, solutes, and other 39
bubbles, often persisting across a broad range of pH and electrolyte conditions [ 19,21,23]. 40
Numerous studies have shown that UFBs can significantly enhance the separation of fine 41
solids, dissolved ions, oils, and organic contaminants through flotation or coagulation 42
techniques [ 16,24]. In the context of particle aggregation, Schubert suggested that UFBs 43
promote bridging between hydrophobic surfaces [ 25], while Liu and coworkers observed 44
stronger agglomeration of coal particles with increasing hydrophobicity [ 26]. Capillary 45
bridging by nanoscale bubbles has been directly reported, with the interaction switching 46
from attractive to repulsive depending on interfacial conditions [ 27,28]. Furthermore, there 47
is evidence of synergy with polymer flocculants, such as cationic polyacrylamide, which 48
enlarges flocs and improves clarification in kaolin systems [ 16,29]. UFBs also interact 49
with particles and interfaces in ways that modify electrokinetic properties and promote 50
aggregation, including bubble-mediated nanoparticle interactions and charge modulation 51
[30]. 52
Numerous studies have reported the effectiveness of UFBs in various applications, 53
including industrial processes, water and wastewater treatment, and as reacting agents in 54
chemical processes [31]. However, the mechanisms underlying their remarkable effective-55
ness remain unclear, prompting this investigation into the flocculation induced by UFBs. A 56
key insight driving this analysis is the significance of colloidal and interfacial aspects of 57
UFBs, particularly their negative charge and their ability to enhance collision frequency by 58
depositing onto substrates. These characteristics likely activate numerous chemical and bio- 59
chemical reactions, making them crucial for effective flocculation. For example, UFBs have 60
been shown to enhance reaction rates in processes such as oxidation and degradation of 61
pollutants, demonstrating their potential as efficient catalyzing agents in various chemical 62
systems. Existing reports have demonstrated charge inversion and reentrant condensation 63
in cationic systems under gas supersaturation [ 32]; however, controlled assessments under 64
defined turbulence and ionic environments that resolve electrokinetic changes and aggrega- 65
tion pathways are scarce. While theDLVO frame work provides abase line forelectrostatic 66
and van derWaals interactions, itisofteninsufficient todescribe aggregation mediated by 67
UFBs. Insystems involv ingUFBs, non-DLVO forces, such ashydropho bicinteractions and 68
gas-mediated capillary bridg ing, areknown toplay adecisive role. This study there fore 69
focuses onexperimen tally quan tifyingthese combined effects, with aparticularemphasis 70
ontheUFBs -specific mech anisms that operatealong side orbeyond classicDLVO theory. 71
Direct force measurements between hydrophobized solids revealed an extra attraction 72
that persists even when a continuous gas phase is excluded, which cannot be explained 73
by DLVO alone [ 33]. Complementary AFM imaging showed that hydrophobized surfaces 74
are decorated by stable surface nanobubbles, providing a physical pathway for capillary 75
bridging over a finite range [34]. 76
In a series of systematic investigations, our research group established end-over-end 77
rotation as a standardized turbulent mixing platform for quantifying collision frequency 78
and rapid coagulation, encompassing a wide range of phenomena from kinetic frame- 79
works to breakup behavior under turbulent conditions [ 35,36]. Within this framework, we 80
mapped the polymer-driven flocculation mechanisms through polyelectrolyte adsorption 81
and bridging on monodisperse polystyrene latex, elucidating how factors such as molecu- 82
Version November 19, 2025 submitted toAppl. Nano 3 of 21
lar weight, charge density, ionic strength, and post-adsorption relaxation influence both 83
mobility and early-stage aggregation [ 37–39]. We subsequently extended this approach to 84
particulate flocculants and demonstrated that adsorption layers formed from sustainable 85
clays, polyelectrolyte complexes, and microgels expand the effective collision radius and 86
accelerate early-stage flocculation within the same turbulent mixing environment [ 40–42]. 87
These findings contribute to a fundamental principle: the deposition of a soft or particulate 88
interfacial layer that extends into the flow effectively increases the geometric collision cross 89
section and fosters bridging interactions, thereby enhancing aggregation.90
Guided by this principle, rather than investigating UFBs as direct flocculants, we as- 91
sess their potential role as bridging and activation agents by depositing them onto cationic 92
colloids to modulate their surface charge, which in turn enhances collision efficiency under 93
standardized turbulence. Amidine latex serves as a well-defined cationic model, and we 94
systematically vary pH, NaCl concentration, and UFBs concentration while maintaining 95
consistent mixing conditions. We focused on pH 6 and pH 9, where UFBs remain stably 96
anionic due to hydroxide adsorption at the gas–water interface [ 43–45], while amidine 97
latex particles possess a positive charge below their isoelectric point (pH 10) [ 46,47]. This 98
setup ensures distinct conditions for charge compensation and patch flocculation. Floc- 99
culation kinetics are quantified using a Coulter counter, while electrophoretic mobility 100
tracks interfacial charge modulation and potential inversion. Additionally, Brownian mo- 101
tion analysis provides insights into the hydrodynamic layer thickness. This integrated 102
design facilitates direct examinations of UFBs-mediated charge modulation and bridg- 103
ing in a cationic colloid. We observe that UFBs solutions can significantly modify the 104
electrophoretic properties of positively charged amidine latex, including charge inversion 105
under specific conditions, with aggregation reaching its peak within a narrow pH range 106
near the isoelectric point, where electrostatic repulsion is minimized. To our knowledge, 107
few studies have comprehensively mapped the combined effects of pH, ionic strength, 108
and UFBs concentration on both electrokinetic and kinetic properties of a well-defined 109
cationic colloid under controlled turbulence. The present dataset addresses this research 110
gap and provides a quantitative foundation for interpreting how charged UFBs interact 111
with charged particles in flocculation-relevant environments.112

Appendix A.2. Ionic strength dependency 464
Ultrafine bubbles (UFBs) in deionized water typically exhibit a net negative charge 465
arising from the preferential adsorption of hydroxide ions (OH−) at the gas–liquid inter- 466
face [ 50,57]. This behavior was reproduced in our measurements, as shown in Fig. A2, 467
where the electrophoretic mobility (EPM) of UFBs is plotted as a function of NaCl concentra- 468
tion at a fixed pH of6.0 ±0.3. At very low ionic strength, the UFBs exhibited a pronounced 469
negative mobility, reflecting the dominance of hydroxyl adsorption. With increasing NaCl

concentration, the magnitude of the negative mobility progressively decreased due to 471
compression of the electrical double layer and screening of interfacial charges. This trend is 472

Appendix A.3. pH dependency 477
Fig. A3 presents the electrophoretic mobility of UFBs, amidine latex (AL) particles, 478
and UFBs–AL mixtures across the pH range 2–12 at a background NaCl concentration of 479
0.1 mM. Compared to AL suspensions alone, the addition of UFBs consistently reduced 480
the measured mobility, shifting values toward less positive or even negative levels. This 481
reduction is attributed to the partial adsorption of anionic UFBs onto the cationic amidine 482
surface, leading to charge masking and, in some cases, compensation. The effect was 483
strongest below the isoelectric point (pH ∼10), where AL particles are most strongly 484
cationic. Because the bulk pH of the suspensions remained stable, these reductions cannot 485
be explained by protonation equilibria, but instead indicate direct electrostatic UFBs–AL 486
interactions. 487
The surface charge of UFBs is known to be highly pH-sensitive [ 31]. In our measure- 488
ments, UFBs remained negatively charged over most of the range, becoming increasingly 489
negative at alkaline pH, but reversed to slightly positive mobility at pH 2. This crossover 490
reflects the competition between H+and OH−ions at the interface: under acidic condi- 491
tions protons dominate, while at higher pH hydroxide adsorption imparts strong negative 492
charge. Such behavior has been confirmed in prior studies of air–water interfaces [ 43,44,61]. 493
Mechanistically, this pH response has been linked to hydration energy differences between 494
protons and hydroxide ions, and to the preferred orientation of interfacial water dipoles, 495
